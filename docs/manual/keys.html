Putting the view back

Three separate resets sit in the View menu, because “put the page back” means different things depending on what you touched:

ItemResetsLeaves alone
Reset rotationthe angleboth flips, zoom, pan
Reset rotation and flipangle and BOTH flipszoom, pan
Reset viewangle, both flips, and refits the page—
Reset rotation does not unmirror. The mirror is the drawing-error check, and you want it to survive straightening a page you had twisted to ink a curve. If the page looks wrong-handed after a reset, you wanted the second item.
The middle one keeps your zoom. That is what makes it usable mid-drawing — it will not throw you back out to a whole page when you are working into a face.
The two flips compose into a half turn, not a double mirror. Horizontal and vertical together leave the page upside down and the RIGHT way round — the handedness is back to normal, so it is not the mirror check any more. Each flip pivots about the centre of the view rather than swinging the page away, and a flip survives a fit (the page stays flipped, and stays centred).
The status bar carries the flip state. mirror for the horizontal flip and mirror V for the vertical, beside the zoom and the angle — both segments show when both are on. The Navigator's two flip buttons light up the same way. Either reset that mentions flip clears both axes: a reset that left the page upside down would be a lie.

Getting the interface out of the way

Tab hides every palette. Shift+Tab also hides the menu bar at the top and the status bar at the bottom — canvas and nothing else. The same keys bring each back, and so do View ▸ Show palettes and View ▸ Show menu and status bars.

“Tab hid my palettes and I don't know why.” That is this feature, and it is a single keypress away from the T and the W you were reaching for. Press Tab again, or Esc, or reopen them from the View menu. Nothing was closed and no layout was lost — the palettes are exactly where you left them.
The menu bar is also the window's title bar. The – □ × buttons and the strip you drag the window by live in it, so while Shift+Tab has it hidden there is nothing to click to close or move the window. Four ways back: Shift+Tab, Esc, Alt+Space (Windows' own Move/Close menu), and Alt+F4.
Neither hide survives a restart — on purpose. The app always starts with its palettes and chrome, whatever state you left it in. Nothing you can press can leave the next launch stranded. The View menu's crop-mark switch is the opposite case and is remembered, because that one is a preference rather than a get-out-of-my-way key.
Neither key fires while you are typing. With a text field focused — a layer name, the Quick Access search, the text tool on canvas — Tab and Esc belong to the text, not to the palettes.

Touch gestures

On a touchscreen, one finger pans and two fingers pinch, twist and drag the canvas. Three tap gestures sit on top of that — and they are all off until you turn them on:

GestureDoesSwitch
Two-finger tapUndo1
Three-finger tapRedo2
Three-finger tap on the NavigatorPut the view back upright and unmirrored (rotation to 0°, flip off) in one gesture4

Switch them on in View ▸ Touch gestures — one entry per gesture, ticked when it is on, and the change takes effect immediately. They are remembered between sessions.

They are also stored as a single number in ui.txt beside the exe (touch_gestures=, the sum of the switches above: 1 for undo only, 3 for both taps, 7 for all three), which is worth knowing only if you are editing that file for some other reason. Editing it while the app is running does nothing — it rewrites the file on exit from what it read at startup.

They are off by default because a resting palm is also “two fingers”. No amount of filtering makes that judgement perfect, so each gesture has its own switch and you decide which ones your hand can be trusted with. A tap only counts if the fingers land together (within 100 ms), stay put (under ~12 px), are fingertip-sized, and are gone again within 300 ms — anything slower or larger is read as a pan, a pinch or a hand.
Rest your palm and the taps stop working until you lift it. That is deliberate: while an extra contact is on the glass the finger count is a lie, so nothing fires. Lift the hand and the next tap works normally.
The Navigator tap needs the Navigator visible. If that palette is closed, or is tabbed behind another one, a three-finger tap there is an ordinary Redo.

Recorded deviations

Ctrl+D is Deselect. CSP uses it for Reselect; we kept Deselect on the plain chord deliberately, and Reselect lives on Ctrl+Shift+D.
Page flipping is Ctrl+PageUp/PageDown and Ctrl+Home/End. CSP's bare comma/period are our sub-tool stepping keys, and bare PageUp/PageDown are zoom bindings — so page navigation took the Ctrl chords.
