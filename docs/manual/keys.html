Modifiers on the canvas

Clip Studio's own set, and they mean the same here. They work whatever tool is in your hand, so none of them costs you the tool.

Zoom in and zoom out land on round numbers. The keys walk a fixed ladder — … 33, 50, 66, 100, 150, 200, 300, 400 % … — rather than multiplying by a wheel notch, so the same key always brings you back to the same scale, and the status line says which one you landed on. The mouse wheel is the continuum: it keeps its own factor in Preferences ▸ Canvas & view ▸ Wheel zoom step. PageUp and PageDown walk the ladder too, so the two ways of saying “zoom in” cannot disagree.
Rotate 90/180/270 are absolute, the arrow keys are relative. View ▸ Rotate 90° puts the page at exactly that angle no matter where it was; - and F9 step by the View rotation step preference (15° shipped). 270° reads back on the status bar as −90°, because the view keeps its angle in the range −180°…180° — same page, same turn, shorter number.

Putting the view back

Three separate resets sit in the View menu, because “put the page back” means different things depending on what you touched:

ItemResetsLeaves alone
Reset rotationthe angleboth flips, zoom, pan
Reset rotation and flipangle and BOTH flipszoom, pan
Reset viewangle, both flips, and refits the page—
Reset rotation does not unmirror. The mirror is the drawing-error check, and you want it to survive straightening a page you had twisted to ink a curve. If the page looks wrong-handed after a reset, you wanted the second item.
The middle one keeps your zoom. That is what makes it usable mid-drawing — it will not throw you back out to a whole page when you are working into a face.
The two flips compose into a half turn, not a double mirror. Horizontal and vertical together leave the page upside down and the RIGHT way round — the handedness is back to normal, so it is not the mirror check any more. Each flip pivots about the centre of the view rather than swinging the page away, and a flip survives a fit (the page stays flipped, and stays centred).
The status bar carries the flip state. mirror for the horizontal flip and mirror V for the vertical, beside the zoom and the angle — both segments show when both are on. The Navigator's two flip buttons light up the same way. Either reset that mentions flip clears both axes: a reset that left the page upside down would be a lie.

Getting the interface out of the way

Tab hides every palette. Shift+Tab also hides the menu bar at the top and the status bar at the bottom — canvas and nothing else. The same keys bring each back, and so do View ▸ Show palettes and View ▸ Show menu and status bars.

“Tab hid my palettes and I don't know why.” That is this feature, and it is a single keypress away from the T and the W you were reaching for. Press Tab again, or Esc, or reopen them from the View menu. Nothing was closed and no layout was lost — the palettes are exactly where you left them.
The menu bar is also the window's title bar. The – □ × buttons and the strip you drag the window by live in it, so while Shift+Tab has it hidden there is nothing to click to close or move the window. Four ways back: Shift+Tab, Esc, Alt+Space (Windows' own Move/Close menu), and Alt+F4.
Neither hide survives a restart — on purpose. The app always starts with its palettes and chrome, whatever state you left it in. Nothing you can press can leave the next launch stranded. The View menu's crop-mark switch is the opposite case and is remembered, because that one is a preference rather than a get-out-of-my-way key.
Neither key fires while you are typing. With a text field focused — a layer name, the Quick Access search, the text tool on canvas — Tab and Esc belong to the text, not to the palettes.

Touch gestures

On a touchscreen, one finger pans and two fingers pinch, twist and drag the canvas. Three tap gestures sit on top of that — and they are all off until you turn them on:

GestureDoesSwitch
Two-finger tapUndo1
Three-finger tapRedo2
Three-finger tap on the NavigatorPut the view back upright and unmirrored (rotation to 0°, flip off) in one gesture4

Switch them on in View ▸ Touch gestures — one entry per gesture, ticked when it is on, and the change takes effect immediately. They are remembered between sessions.

They are also stored as a single number in ui.txt beside the exe (touch_gestures=, the sum of the switches above: 1 for undo only, 3 for both taps, 7 for all three), which is worth knowing only if you are editing that file for some other reason. Editing it while the app is running does nothing — it rewrites the file on exit from what it read at startup.

They are off by default because a resting palm is also “two fingers”. No amount of filtering makes that judgement perfect, so each gesture has its own switch and you decide which ones your hand can be trusted with. A tap only counts if the fingers land together (within 100 ms), stay put (under ~12 px), are fingertip-sized, and are gone again within 300 ms — anything slower or larger is read as a pan, a pinch or a hand.
Rest your palm and the taps stop working until you lift it. That is deliberate: while an extra contact is on the glass the finger count is a lie, so nothing fires. Lift the hand and the next tap works normally.
The Navigator tap needs the Navigator visible. If that palette is closed, or is tabbed behind another one, a three-finger tap there is an ordinary Redo.

Hold a tool key to borrow it

Every tool key works two ways, like CSP: tap to switch and stay, hold to borrow — work, release the key, and you are back on the tool you had, same sub tool, same size. Pen → hold R → rotate the canvas → release → still the pen. A press counts as a hold once it passes a quarter second, or as soon as you draw or drag while it is down — so even a quick flick with the key held returns. Clicking a different tool in the palette while holding cancels the return: your click wins.

Your own keys — keys.json

Put a keys.json next to the exe to bind (or rebind) any command the command palette (Ctrl+K) can run by name:

{
  "ctrl+1": "Snap to rulers",
  "f2": "Cut",
  "ctrl+shift+t": "Transform"
}

Chord on the left — ctrl/shift/alt in any order, one key last (letters, digits, f1–f12, [ ] , . and the usual named keys). Command label on the right, exactly as the palette shows it (case doesn't matter). Your bindings win over the built-in table, and a chord only fires on its exact modifiers. The file is read at launch; a line that doesn't resolve is reported in the status bar and the rest still bind.

Aiming a key at a tool, a tab, or one sub tool

Instead of a command, the right-hand side can name a place in the Sub Tool list. Write tool: and then the tool, optionally the group caption, optionally the row:

{
  "u": "tool: Frame border",
  "shift+u": "tool: Figure / Saturated line",
  "ctrl+shift+u": "tool: Figure / Direct draw / Ellipse",
  "j": ["tool: Fill", "tool: Auto select"]
}

The three lengths are three different promises. One name — the tool, and it lands on the group and sub tool you last used there. Two — that group, on whichever of its rows you last used. Three — that exact row, always. All three read the names the Sub Tool palette shows you, and case doesn't matter.

A list puts several targets on one key: press it again and it walks them in the order you wrote, which is how Clip Studio puts three tools on one letter. The walk has no memory of its own — it looks at where you actually are and moves on from there, so clicking a tool in the palette between presses never leaves the key out of step. Our own T (Text ⇄ Balloon) and E (Eraser ⇄ Pen) are exactly this, written in the built-in table.

The tool a key lands on is remembered per group and per tool in ui.txt, so it survives closing the app: leave Figure in Saturated line on Dark burst, and that is where F puts you tomorrow.

Recorded deviations

Three tool letters moved. CSP stacks Figure, Frame border and Ruler all on U and Gradient with Fill on G; here U is Frame border alone, Figure is F and Gradient is V. B is a second Pen key rather than CSP's Brush. If your hands want CSP's stacking back, one keys.json line does it: "u": ["tool: Frame border", "tool: Figure"] — a list on one key is exactly the repeat-press cycle CSP uses.
Ctrl+D is Deselect. CSP uses it for Reselect; we kept Deselect on the plain chord deliberately, and Reselect lives on Ctrl+Shift+D.
Page flipping is Ctrl+PageUp/PageDown and Ctrl+Home/End. CSP's bare comma/period are our sub-tool stepping keys, and bare PageUp/PageDown are zoom bindings — so page navigation took the Ctrl chords.
Layer colour and Clip to layer below have no built-in chord. They are palette commands (Layer colour on/off, Clip to layer below) that act on the layer selected when you press the key, so bind them yourself if you came from CSP's Ctrl+B / Ctrl+Alt+G. Merge selected layers and Release folder are the same shape — they act on the palette's selection, so they too are palette commands without a built-in chord. Add these two lines to keys.json for CSP's own chords: 
"shift+alt+e": "Merge selected layers (combine the palette selection)",
"ctrl+shift+g": "Release folder (ungroup, children step out)"
Merge selected takes the rows you Ctrl- or Shift-clicked in the palette (the refusals are Merge-down's: no folders, frames, balloons, text, vector, locked or clipped rows, and nothing spanning a folder edge). Release folder works from the folder row or from any layer inside it.
Ctrl+K is itself rebindable. The command palette opens through a command like everything else (Command palette), so it can move — including onto CSP's Ctrl+K-shaped habits from another editor.
