Layers & masks

Blending modes

The list is not in Clip Studio's order. Modes sit in the order they were built — Normal, Multiply, Screen, Add, Subtract first, then Overlay through Color, then the burn/dodge/light family last. So Color burn is at the bottom of the list, nowhere near Color dodge. The list keeps its order because saved files and your own muscle memory both count positions in it.
"Brightness" is what other apps call "Luminosity". It takes the upper layer's brightness and the lower layer's colour — the exact opposite of the Color mode two rows above it. It is not a tonal correction and it has no slider.
Glow dodge is our own shape, not a copy of Clip Studio's. Clip Studio has never published what its glow modes compute. Ours does the thing the mode is for: unlike Color dodge, which cannot lighten a pure black pixel at all, Glow dodge lifts black. If you are matching a file made in Clip Studio, expect this one mode to differ; the other twenty-six are the standard formulas.
There is no "Add (Glow)" because Add already is it. Clip Studio splits Add into two, differing only on part-transparent pixels. Ours is the stronger of the two, so a Clip Studio file using Add (Glow) lands on the right mode; a file using the plainer Add will read slightly brighter here where the layer is semi-transparent.
Hard mix, Darker color and Lighter color have hard edges by design. Hard mix snaps every channel to 0 or full, and the two colour compares switch the whole colour at once. Gradients under them band instead of blending — that is the mode working, not a rendering fault.
Another app opening your .ora may not see these modes. Fifteen of the twenty-seven use standard names other editors know. The rest (Linear burn, Glow dodge, Vivid light, Linear light, Pin light, Hard mix, Divide, Darker color, Lighter color, Add, Subtract) are ours; a foreign editor falls back to Normal for those layers. Your own files reload correctly — this only affects handing a .ora to someone else.

Mask editing is a mode that arms

Clicking a layer's mask square routes your brush to the mask. While mask edit is armed, every stroke paints the mask's coverage instead of the layer's pixels. The mode disarms safely at the start of a stroke when it is no longer valid (the mask was deleted or baked) — those strokes drop instead of painting the layer by accident. Click the square again to leave the mode.

What undo does not cover

Mask strokes are not undoable. Painting on a mask writes the mask field directly; there is no undo group for it. If a mask stroke went wrong, re-paint it — or clear the mask (Layer ▸ Layer Mask ▸ Clear) and redo it.
Baking a mask leaves the mask outside the undo bracket. Layer ▸ Layer Mask ▸ Apply commits the mask into the pixels as one undo step — but undoing that step restores the pixels and not the mask you spent time painting. Keep the layer's .ora saved if the mask matters.

Screentone

Dot position moves the DOTS, not the drawing. The two "Dot position" bars slide the halftone lattice underneath art that stays exactly where it is — that is what they are for. Two tone layers at the same frequency and angle interfere and print as ugly bands; nudging one of them a few pixels breaks it. The lattice repeats, so a large offset eventually lands back on the same dots and appears to do nothing — a nudge of a few pixels is the whole intent.
"Specified density" ignores what you painted. With it on, every dot is the same size no matter how dark the ink underneath is — the flat "40 % tone" fill. Alpha still decides where: transparent areas stay empty. "Brightness of image" is identical to "Colour of image" on grey and black art and only differs on coloured art, where it weighs blue as darker than green.
Past 50 % density every shape turns inside out. Below half, the shape grows from the middle of each cell; above half, you are looking at shrinking holes of paper at the cell corners — which is how real screentone behaves. Star and Asterisk turn into round holes up there, so a very dark "star tone" stops looking like stars.
Noise has no lattice, and Asterisk is approximate. Noise is a random dither, so it cannot moiré against anything and its grain follows the Frequency bar rather than a Size control. Every shape solves its own size from the density you asked for, but Asterisk's size law is fitted rather than solved, so its density is close rather than exact. If a printed density has to be exact, use Dots, Square, Ellipse, Lozenge, Cross or Lines.
Posterize is not on live tone Fill layers. The steps control appears on the layer effect panel only. A live Fill layer already draws one flat density, so there is no ramp for it to flatten.
View ▸ Show tone area tints whole 64 px blocks. It marks every toned region of every visible layer green so a forgotten scrap of tone shows up before the page prints. The tint is deliberately coarse — a three-pixel scrap lights up its whole block, because a scrap you can see is the point. It is a view only: nothing is saved, exported or undoable.

Filters

Filters have no preview — they apply when you press Apply. Filter ▸ Blur ▸ Gaussian blur, Motion blur and Filter ▸ Effect ▸ Mosaic all commit straight to the layer. There is nothing on canvas showing what the numbers will do beforehand, so the way to judge a setting is to apply it, look, and Ctrl+Z if it is wrong. Each filter is exactly one undo step no matter how much of the page it touched.
A filter runs on the active layer only, and stops at the selection. Not on the merged image, not on a folder — one raster layer, the one highlighted in the Layer palette. With a selection active the change is confined to it, but the blur still reads pixels from outside the marquee, so the edge of the selection does not darken. A folder, a text/balloon/frame layer or a locked layer refuses the filter and the status bar says so.
Blur spreads ink past where the drawing was. A stroke near the edge of its layer blurs outwards into empty space, so the layer covers a little more than it did — up to the blur's own radius. It stops at the paper edge: a filter will not push content out beyond the canvas.
"Lock transparent pixels" turns a blur into a colour-only blur. With the lock on, the alpha of every pixel is put back exactly as it was after the filter runs, so the silhouette does not move and only the colours inside it soften. That is the trick for softening flats without fattening the line they sit under.
Gaussian strength is approximate at the bottom of the slider. The blur is built from three box passes (which is why it is fast at large radii), and those passes come in whole pixels — so strengths between about 1 and 2 px round to the same few results instead of increasing smoothly. Above ~3 px it tracks the number. Mosaic cells are anchored to the paper's top-left corner, not to your selection, so mosaicking two areas separately keeps one continuous grid.

Solos

Alt+click an EYE solos the layer. Every other layer's eye goes off; Alt+click the same eye again and the exact previous visibility comes back. The snapshot lives per page — switching pages forgets it (the second press then starts a fresh solo).
The Reference checkbox has its own solo. Alt+click the "Reference layer" checkbox solos the REFERENCE set (every other layer's reference flag clears) — a different solo from the eye's, and the two do not interact.

The Correction menu

A correction is baked into the pixels. Brightness/Contrast, Hue/Saturation/Luminosity, Posterization, Reverse gradient and Binarization rewrite the layer's pixels. It is one undo step while the file is open — but once you save and close, the original tones are gone. Duplicate the layer first if you might want them back. There is no correction layer you can re-open and re-edit.
It corrects the ACTIVE layer, not what you see. The canvas shows every layer composited; a correction touches exactly one. Binarizing a page whose lineart sits above a white base changes the lineart layer only — the white stays white because it is a different layer.
A selection bounds it. With a selection active, pixels outside it are left byte-for-byte alone and a partly selected pixel gets a proportional amount of the correction — a feathered selection fades the correction out along its edge. Deselect first if you meant the whole layer.
Transparent pixels stay transparent, always. No correction here changes alpha, Binarization included: it drives colour to pure black or pure white and leaves opacity untouched, so binarizing scanned lineart does not flood the page with white. CSP's "Leave transparency" switch has no off position here.
The preview is the real canvas, and anything else abandons it. While the dialog is open the correction is painted into the actual pixels so you judge it at full size. That means nothing else may touch the document: any other command — a menu item, a shortcut, switching layers or tabs — puts the pixels back and closes the dialog, and a pen stroke is refused with a note in the status bar. Press Apply before you go anywhere. Untick Preview to see the untouched layer without closing the dialog.
Reverse gradient applies immediately. It has no settings, so there is no dialog and no preview — the menu item is the whole operation. Undo is the way back.

Filtering the stack

You cannot drag rows to reorder while a filter is on. With rows missing, a drop line drawn between two visible rows would land the layer somewhere you cannot see, so the palette turns reordering off and says "reorder off" beside the count. Clear the filter to reorder. Everything else — the eye, rename, select, delete, the folder triangle — keeps working on exactly the layer named in the row.
A filtered stack is FLAT. Collapsed folders stop hiding their children while a filter is on, otherwise a match inside a shut folder would look like a filter that found nothing. The folder triangles go back to doing their job the moment the filter is cleared.
The active layer always shows, match or not. It is the layer your pen is on; a stack that hides it is worse than one extra row. It is included in the "n of m" count.
"This frame folder" means the koma holding the ACTIVE layer — the whole block, header and children, found by walking up from wherever the active layer sits. With the active layer in no frame folder at all the filter matches nothing, and the count row says so rather than leaving you with a blank palette.

Palette colours

A folder wears the topmost palette colour from inside it. A folder with no colour of its own shows the colour of the highest layer within it that has one, so a collapsed folder still tells you what it holds. Give the folder a colour of its own and that one wins — which is also how you stop a child's colour showing on the folder. Nesting is followed all the way down; a folder with nothing coloured inside it keeps a bare strip.
Select layer is a sub tool of Operation, and it finds layers by pointing at the page. Pick Operation ▸ Select layer and click a pixel: the Layer palette jumps to whichever layer drew it. It answers with the TOPMOST layer that has ink there, so on a finished page the answer is usually a tone or the text — which is what the Exclude switches in Tool Property are for. Draft, text and locked layers are skipped by default; fill/tone layers are not, because sometimes the tone IS what you were pointing at.
Select layer ignores hidden layers whatever the Exclude switches say, and needs real ink, not a fringe. A layer you cannot see is never the answer. Neither is a pixel less than about 10% opaque — the soft edge of an antialiased line one layer up would otherwise win every click against the solid line underneath it. If the click reports nothing found, either the excludes ate every candidate or you clicked the paper.

Border effect

The outline grows OUTSIDE the drawing, into space the layer never painted. Layer Property ▸ Border effect wraps a keyline round the layer's own alpha — the white line that lets a character read against a tone — so the layer now covers more of the page than its pixels do, by up to the thickness you set. It stops at the paper edge: outline that would fall off the page is never drawn and never exported.
Nothing is painted, and it follows the drawing. The pixels underneath are untouched — turn the effect off and the layer is byte-for-byte what it was. Keep drawing with it on and the outline is round the new ink on the next frame; erase and it retreats. Thickness is in canvas pixels (not mm) and caps at 32.
Half-transparent ink does not get an outline. A pixel seeds the keyline only at 50 % alpha or more, the same rule the text engine's フチ uses. So the soft tail of an airbrushed stroke is simply not there as far as the outline is concerned, and a layer drawn entirely at low opacity gets no keyline at all — raise the ink, not the thickness.
On a tone layer the keyline wraps the DOTS. The effect is derived after the screentone, so it outlines what the layer shows rather than the grey you painted — each dot gets its own rim. The same is true of a live Fill layer. Folders refuse the effect outright: a folder has no alpha of its own to grow from, and the status bar says so.

Layer colour, sub colour and the mono preview

Layer colour and sub colour are the two ends of one ramp. The main colour replaces the layer's black, the sub colour replaces its white, and everything between is mixed by brightness — the two-tone rough-draft look. Sub colour only appears once a layer colour is set, because on its own it means nothing. Leaving it off is exactly the same picture as setting it to white.
"Preview as" is the screen only — it never reaches the exported page. Grey and Mono (1-bit) answer "would this hold up in print?" without converting a pixel. The export, and the colour the fill and wand tools sample, both ignore it. Layer colour and sub colour are the opposite: those are real rendering and they do print.
Mono preview thresholds coverage as well as value, and that is the point. At 1-bit there are no half-covered pixels, so anything under 50 % alpha vanishes and everything above it goes fully solid — the antialiased edge you have been drawing against disappears and you see the hard, aliased edge a 1-bit page really prints. A preview that kept the soft edge would hide the exact problem it exists to show. The threshold is fixed at 50 %.
Only the border effect is undoable. Ctrl+Z steps through border-effect changes; layer colour, sub colour and the preview mode are presentation switches with no undo step, like the eye — click them back. All four are saved with the file.

Paper

The paper is not a layer, and it is under all of them. View ▸ Paper. There is exactly one per page, you cannot draw on it, move it, delete it or reorder it — so it is a property of the document rather than a row in the stack. It has two knobs and they behave differently on purpose: the colour is part of the drawing (a cream page exports cream, and changing it is undoable), and Show paper is a view switch like a layer's eye (not undoable).
Turning the paper off does NOT export a transparent PNG. With the paper hidden the canvas shows the transparency checker wherever the page is transparent — that is what it is for: a missed spot inside a flat fill is invisible against white and obvious against a checker. It is a check you switch on, not an export mode, so every export still writes the paper colour underneath. If you want a transparent PNG, that is a separate export option, not this switch.
The checker does not zoom. Its squares stay the same size on screen at 12 % and at 800 %, because it belongs to the window and not to the drawing. If it scaled with the page it would read as a big grey pattern you had painted.
Paper colours are a short preset list plus your current colour. View ▸ Paper offers white, cream, newsprint, two greys and black; for anything else, pick the colour in the Color palette and choose "Use the current drawing colour". Black lineart on a mid grey paper is the quick way to check whether your darks are carrying.
